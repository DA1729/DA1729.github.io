# Abstract Structures for Relating Physical Theories

Often in mathematics, a problem that is difficult or impossible to solve within a particular formulation becomes approachable/solvable when viewed from a different level of abstraction. This could happen because abstraction reveals relations that were earlier masked. In other words, it exposes implicit assumptions.

A very important viewpoint is that **difficulties may not arise from the intrinsic complexity of the problem, but rather from the mathematical setting in which we have chosen to formulate it.**

Now, I have been thinking way too much about this while studying General Relativity and am aware of the problems in Quantum Gravity. I will deliberately refrain from discussing how good approaches like String Theory, Causal Set Theory, etc., are, simply because I am not well qualified to do so. This puts me in a position to construct approaches without being influenced by their technical nuances. This has its own merits and demerits, but I don't have to worry about those.

What I am about to discuss in this blog is nicely captured by the following speculation:
> Could some of the difficulties involved in reconciling major physical theories arise, or at least partly, from the mathematical formulations/regime in which we attempt to establish their compatibility?

I am not going to discuss how this viewpoint might resolve quantum gravity. Neither am I going to put out points asserting the failure of current approaches. I am only going to discuss what such a change in perspective might actually involve.

## Wiring analogy

Imagine two enormously complicated systems that we want to connect or wire together. One might ask, why wire them at all? Because we somehow know that wiring them together will result in a richer, more elegant single system when viewed as a whole. It's a safe assumption because, again, we somehow know that unifying relativity and quantum theory will give us an elegant theory.

One approach is to investigate their individual components and establish connections between corresponding parts. We may successfully connect certain components, only to discover later that these choices are incompatible with connections we need to establish elsewhere. This puts us in a position where we would have to reconsider earlier decisions, potentially repeating this process many times. This spits out an obvious fact: **compatibility of individual components does not necessarily guarantee the compatibility of the combined system.**

Now let's invoke our mathematical abstraction lens. Rather than attempting to construct a relationship through a sequence of local adjustments, we could investigate the mathematical requirements of the relationship itself.

A structural understanding of the relationship might impose constraints on the entire construction, allowing the proposed connections between individual components to be tested against those constraints. The aim would be to establish a mathematical setting in which subsequent technical investigations are informed by the structure of the relationship, rather than allowing each local result to determine how the overall connection should develop independently.

## Investigating relationship before individual realisations

Say we have two mathematical structures, $A$ and $B$.

A natural approach is to understand their individual properties through their elements and the relationships between them, and then attempt to infer a relationship between them.

Note that this is not a mathematical restriction; in fact, it allows us to reverse this order of actions. We could begin by investigating the possible mathematical structure of a relationship between $A$ and $B$.

For instance, if we consider a mapping: $$F: A \rightarrow B,$$ we might investigate its possible structural properties before specifying its action on individual elements. Such properties could constrain the structures it connects.

Once we have specified such constraints, we can investigate specific elements, operations, and mathematical constructions. The approach is schematically given by: $$\boxed{\text{Investigate structural relationships}\rightarrow\text{Derive constraints}\rightarrow\text{Investigate particular realisations}}.$$

One can clearly see that the motivation is not merely to replace calculations with abstract notation. But rather, to find mathematical relationships that make subsequent calculations more systematic, reveal nontrivial/counter-intuitive consequences, and provide a way to test whether observations made in particular cases are compatible with the larger structure.
