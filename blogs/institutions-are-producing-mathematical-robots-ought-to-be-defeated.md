# Institutions are Producing Mathematical Robots Ought to Be Defeated

I genuinely think that mathematical research is facing a critical problem with how mathematics is generally taught to bachelor's and master's students.

Almost every research frontier I encounter is not just a single subject. It is a combination of multiple fields, and I think this goes for many other areas of research presently. Say someone is working in an area where they need algebra, geometry, number theory, and perhaps topology. These are already huge subjects in their own rights.

Now consider the traditional, ideal-student approach. You study a book, attend all the lectures, work through proofs, try to prove some theorems yourself, and solve multiple problems. You don't stick to a single book either. You read several books, look at different approaches, and keep revising what you have studied.

All the nice, good-student practices.

But there is a problem with how much of your time this can consume, particularly when what you eventually want to do is research across these fields. And I am concerned that the downside might go beyond just the time spent.

## Two kinds of mathematical confidence

We can roughly distinguish between two mathematical abilities. Let's call them vertical mathematical confidence and horizontal mathematical confidence.

The vertical one is fairly familiar. Someone studies number theory, learns increasingly sophisticated techniques, works through proofs, and solves difficult exercises. They develop an intuition for which methods are likely to work on particular number-theoretic problems. They become really good at navigating that theoretical framework.

The horizontal one is when someone encounters a problem in number theory and recognizes that its essential structure resembles something in algebraic geometry, harmonic analysis, or perhaps theoretical computer science. They can then restructure the problem and express it in a language where useful tools are available.

I am not claiming these are mutually exclusive abilities. In fact, developing the second one requires some amount of the first. You need to understand the language you are trying to translate. Otherwise, what exactly are you connecting?

My argument is that the traditional curriculum explicitly trains the first ability and focuses too much on it. Like, a lot.

The second one is mostly treated as something students will eventually acquire through mathematical maturity, research experience, and just spending enough time around mathematics.

We have carefully structured sequences of textbooks, lectures, assignments, and examinations for the first ability. But learning to recognize which mathematical framework a problem should be placed in is largely left to what happens afterwards.

And at this point, I'll stop being a bit modest and say that I think the ability to translate between different fields will become more important than the ability to independently carry out every part of the resulting technical argument.

## Where do your hours go?

Consider two students who each have a certain number of hours to spend. Say 10,000 hours, just to give the example a number.

The first student follows the textbook definition of a good student. They spend a large portion of those hours working through several advanced textbooks in number theory, carefully reproducing proofs, trying to find alternate proofs, solving exercises and assignments, scoring well in traditional examinations, and revisiting difficult problem sets.

The second student spends fewer hours developing that same level of technical proficiency and allocates a substantial amount of time to understanding how number-theoretic problems interact with algebraic geometry, representation theory, and other areas of mathematics.

Again, the second student still needs technical proficiency. I am not suggesting that someone can skip understanding number theory and somehow become good at connecting it to everything else.

But the allocation of effort is different.

The first approach produces someone who is exceptionally good at navigating a particular theoretical framework. The second aims to produce someone who is better at recognizing which framework should be used, or how a problem should be reformulated, to take the research further.

Now, these are not just hours on a calendar. Think about where your freshest, most focused hours go. Where do you invest the mental effort with which you could be forming your own picture of these subjects?

I do not want to occupy all my mental bandwidth with technical nuances. I really don't want to. Because that gets in the way of the bigger picture I am trying to form.

My hypothesis is that spending too much time proving increasingly specialized results inside one framework can leave you with too little time and attention to understand its relationships with other frameworks. I have observed something like this in people around me, and I think it is a reasonable hypothesis to put forward. I don't think I need to wait for a rigorous social-scientific experiment before raising the question.

## What if these habits become a limitation?

Say someone spends four years in their bachelor's and two years in their master's. That is six years spent getting familiar with these good-student practices. Even if they do a few research projects, the majority of their time may still go into working through textbooks, reproducing proofs, solving assignments, and preparing for examinations.

Now, six years is a substantial amount of time to spend practicing a particular way of thinking and being rewarded for it. Meanwhile, the available tools are changing, and so are the questions about where we should put our effort and how we should reason about things.

My concern is that by the time this student actually starts working as a full-time mathematician, they might struggle to even recognize what is required and what is important. Translating between multiple complex fields and setting up connections between them might feel like something alien. Not just something they haven't practiced enough, but something they don't quite know how to approach as mathematical work.

For a rough linguistic analogy, think about encountering unfamiliar vowel sounds in a language like Icelandic after spending almost all your life hearing and speaking English. You might struggle to hear the distinctions, let alone pronounce them. The sounds you are familiar with do not necessarily prepare you to notice every distinction in another language.

I am not saying those sounds are impossible to learn, or that this analogy proves anything about mathematical education. But it gets at the concern: being very familiar with one way of doing something does not mean another way will be immediately accessible to you. You may have to deliberately learn to notice what you were not noticing before.

Similarly, a student could become exceptionally good at the mathematics they have been trained to do, while finding it difficult to see the kinds of connections their research actually needs. And if we leave this entirely to whatever mathematical maturity they are supposed to acquire later, we are assuming that the transition will more or less take care of itself.

That is what genuinely concerns me. A very bright student with huge mathematical potential could possibly become blinded to what is required for research and mathematical advancement, precisely because they spent so many years doing everything a good student was supposed to do.

## What problem sets actually train

One response would be that solving difficult problems already teaches you to think differently, take unconventional approaches, and be creative.

Yeah, it does. But there are different things being put under the word creativity here.

Consider an extremely difficult exercise in algebraic number theory. It might require a very clever argument. But before starting, you already know something quite useful: it appears in a chapter on algebraic number theory.

That context has already done some work for you. It gives you a fairly strong idea of what sort of tools might be relevant, even if finding the right application is difficult.

Now consider an unfamiliar research problem. You may not know whether the useful perspective is algebraic, geometric, analytic, combinatorial, or something else entirely.

The difficulty might not be finding an unconventional way to use a technique. It might be realizing that the problem has been expressed in the wrong mathematical language for what you want to do with it.

I do not think conventional problem sets train these abilities equally well.

My first thought was that if someone designed problems that really required students to discover connections across all these huge subjects, they would essentially be giving them research questions. Solving them would itself be a research contribution.

But there is a useful distinction here. The connection does not have to be new to mathematics. It can be new to the student.

For instance, a student could study a known result and be asked why a geometric interpretation reveals something that is difficult to see through arithmetic methods. The contribution has already been made, but the student still has to understand why changing the language helps.

We could have entire courses around rediscovering known mathematical connections. Why should progressing through the theorems of one subject be the default structure for almost everything?

## AI changes this calculation

We also have to keep in mind that AI is accelerating mathematical research. I am not just talking about using it to polish papers. I mean assistance with proofs, methods, and different approaches to solving a problem.

And I am not assuming that papers written with this assistance are weak. Part of what motivates this argument is seeing work that I think is genuinely good.

There is more work to keep up with, while the range of subjects one might need to understand is already enormous.

AI also seems to be getting particularly good at working through proof problems where the relevant framework is relatively well specified.

Say I am trying to prove something that is almost entirely about number theory, but I eventually want to use the result alongside some algebraic geometry primitives. The traditional good-student approach would train me really well to work on that number-theoretic proof.

But if AI can help with that part, I think it makes sense to spend somewhat less time training to do every such step independently, and more time understanding why the result might be useful in the larger construction.

Of course, I still need enough mathematical judgment to understand and check the argument. Otherwise, I don't know what I am actually using.

But understanding and verifying an argument is not the same demand as independently inventing every technical step in it.

The educational objective could therefore change. Alongside learning to construct difficult proofs, we should explicitly train people to recognize structural relationships, identify useful questions, decide which tools are needed, and bring together arguments whose correctness they can evaluate.

That is still mathematical work.

## Do we have to learn everything in textbook order?

A textbook usually develops a theory in a logical order. Definitions come first, then results that depend on them, and then more sophisticated results.

That makes sense for constructing the theory rigorously. But does it follow that this is always the best order for a future researcher to encounter its ideas?

I can understand something about why a theory exists, what problems it addresses, and where it connects to another subject before I can independently prove all its difficult results.

That picture can then tell me which technical details I actually need to study next.

Instead, we often proceed as though we must finish acquiring the machinery of one subject before we are allowed to seriously think about what it might do elsewhere. Given how large these subjects are, that is a fairly expensive expectation.

---

I am not saying proof-writing and problem-solving should disappear. They are part of how we develop mathematical judgment in the first place. I am questioning why they should continue to consume such a large proportion of an advanced student's independent study time.

And I will put the stronger version of my argument here as well: taken too far, the first approach seems suited to producing what we might have called a clerk 50 or 60 years ago. Someone highly trained to carry out difficult work within a framework, while the choice of framework and direction is made elsewhere.

Given the technological changes happening now, going deeper and deeper into a particular subject purely to perform all its technical work yourself increasingly seems to me like something one might do as a hobby, for enjoyment.

There is nothing wrong with that. I enjoy studying mathematics too. But enjoyment and the advancement of mathematical knowledge are not automatically the same objective.

If the objective is research, then I think our institutions need to reconsider what they are training us to become exceptionally good at.
