## The core of the multiplicity hypothesis

For me, the core of the previous post, or the Linguistic Multiplicity -- Abstraction Hypothesis, and as I am calling it now: the linguistic motivation, is the following: $$\text{representation} \neq \text{represented object}.$$

Or, more cautiously, $$\text{a representation need not exhaust the thing represented.}$$

Now, knowledge of a single representation (I am to formalize all of these inferences, but that's going to take time) may encourage identification: $$X \approx A_X.$$

Further, appearance of another representation disrupts this: $A_X \neq B_X$, yet both are somehow associated with $X$.

This produces the "pressure" to distinguish: $$X \neq A_X, X \neq B_X.$$

## New perspective for "abstraction"

I believe this is the most interesting thing I have thought of in a long time.

Instead of defining abstraction as "thinking without words," perhaps: $$\text{abstraction} \sim \text{recognition of what survives the representational change}.$$

Suppose $r$ is a representation and: $$T: r \rightarrow r'$$is some assumed transformation of representation (not a very load-bearing assumption tbf). Then, I am talking about a property $F$, such that: $$F(r) = F(T(r))$$

Now, this suggests: $$\text{abstract content} \approx \text{content invariant under an appropriate class of transformations}.$$Note that I am not using the approximate symbol to represent a little difference between their values, but rather how confident I am with my definition.

I also used, "appropriate class of transformation," because I don't want to assert something very strong for now, although, for me, it's very tempting to write "for all transformations."

## Transformed notion for $R$

In the previous blog, I defined: $$R = \text{capacity/tendency to manipulate structures independently of verbal labels.}$$Possible later reinterpretation: $$R = \text{sensitivity to invariants across changes of representation.}$$

This avoids the following overly crude opposition: $$\text{verbal vs. nonverbal}$$

A mathematician may think entirely using symbols and still be dumb. A better distinction as opposed to the one above might be: $$\text{representationally-bounded} \leftrightarrow \text{representationally-flexible}.$$

## Loophole in the linguistic representation's framing

I do not endorse critique statements following the spirit of what's in this section, but if one frames it as a loophole of the framing, then I am all in. This really provides me substance for what I am trying to compile and it's very interesting.

Say, I design language B, from A, just by changing the alphabet symbols and keeping everything exactly the same. Then technically, $$A_X \neq B_X,$$but nothing conceptually substantial may have changed (I am speculating symbols might have some psychological effect, but let's not care about that for now). This allows me to assert: $$\text{number of representations} \neq \text{useful representational multiplicity.}$$Maybe, eventually, I'll define, and let's only hint at it for now: $$D(P_i, P_j)$$measuring how differently two representational systems organize or expose the same structure.

## Representations

I am just going to write out this, which again, I don't want to settle it as defined, but only as a representation of the direction I could look in: $$\mathcal{P}(X) = \\{P_i(X)\\}_{i \in I}.$$This is the "family of representations of $X$."

Then, there may be transformations: $$T_{ij}: \mathcal{P}_i(X) \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{P}_j(X).$$The interesting object may not merely be $X$, or the $\mathcal{P}_i$'s, but the entire network: $$(\mathcal{P}(X), \\{T_{ij}\\}).$$

Interesting questions to ask given this:
- What relations between $\mathcal{P}_i(X)$ and $\mathcal{P}_j(X)$ justify saying that both represent the "same" $X$?
- What structure is preserved by $T_{ij}$.
